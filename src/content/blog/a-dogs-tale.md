It has been nearly four years since my son (nine years old at the time) and were forced to move out of our family home down to Florida; forced to leave behind our family dog, who stayed with my brother and sister-in-law. 

When we said goodbye, the intention was to return within two weeks. We had no idea that this would be the last time we would see our beloved Rosie for years. While we were down in Florida, an unforeseen family event, to put it mildly, led us no choice but to move south permanently.

As the years passed, we settled into a new Rosie-less life as Rosie continued to live with my brother and his wife. We rescued another dog (our precious Shea), dug our roots into the Florida sand, and acclimated into a new normal. But how long does '"normal" ever really last?

Due to a series of events that I'm not going to get into now, but might address at another time, my brother and sister-in-law became unable to care for Rosie any longer. This meant two things: Rosie was soon to be homeless, and two, we were the only solution. I would be lying if I said I wasn't pumped to finally be reunited with our Rosie girl, despite the circumstances.

Rosie has always had an aggression problem. She is not a mean dog, but she is terrified of strangers. She has nipped people in the past, and it's honestly a miracle that she has never been flagged by animal control.

Since we had not seen her in so long, I worried that she would not recognize us. My sister made the long hall down to Florida, Rosie in tow for the 15 hour trip. When they finally arrived, with our fingers crossed, we hoped and prayed that her nose would do its thing and everything would click like no time had passed at all.

She recognized me immediately, waddling her way over, tail wagging, nose snorting from the effort. And then she saw my son, who in four years, had aged into a hulking teenager, at least a foot and a half taller, and surely a new manly scent. She immediately tried to lunge at him. If it weren't for us having her muzzled and harnessed (which I have since learned is one of the worst things we could have done), he would have become puppy chow (not good for her diet at all).

I was amazed at how much her physical health had declined in the few years since we were last together. She more closely resembled a baked potato than an aging pit bull. She had gained at least 20 lbs. and could barely waddle more than 20 feet without getting out of breath. Her movements were hesitant. Unconfident. Her ailing hips caused her to walk like a penguin.

I had promised myself that if either my son or Shea were in any danger, I would not be allowing Rosie to come live with us, but the only other option was to put her down. She was too aggressive for rescues and kennels, and my parents were not physically strong enough to take ownership of her. So, it was up to me to figure this out.

Putting her down, when she was still able to walk, and cuddle, and eat, and go potty, was completely out of the question. We needed another solution. So I called a few local dog behavioralists.

The first trainer I called was one of the rudest business owners I've ever spoken to, but the second trainer I spoke to was clearly the right choice. She spoke to me on the phone for an hour, prepping me for what to expect, what tools I should buy, and rightfully, but respectfully told me the harness and muzzle had to go. She suggested an arrangement that would promise safety until we were able to meet, involving Rosie and my room with me, and Shea sequestered in my son's room. We set an appointment for the the following week, which was today.

Dog training is much more physically taxing than you might expect, especially when the trainer uses a modified version of Schutzhund training, the same method they use to train police dogs. Today was all about basic control, so it involved a lot of tugging and assertive vocal reprimands, followed by an appropriate amount of "good girl." We were both spent by the end of the session.

I feel much more in control of the situation than I did yesterday, which I guess is the point. Rosie was not thrilled with the experience, but she was clearly starting to catch on that the hierarchy has changed.

I'm cautiously optimistic that we're going in the right direction. The aggressive behavior just seems so baked in, but this whole exercise is about behavior modification. From the progress we made today, there is a glimmer of hope.
